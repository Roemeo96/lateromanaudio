Engineering Weltgeist

Weltgeist began with a simple idea: what if the dynamics of an instrument could control effects that normally require an expression pedal?

Turning that idea into an actual circuit was considerably less simple.

This page documents some of the engineering decisions, unexpected behaviours and measurements that shaped Weltgeist. It is not intended as a complete schematic or construction guide, but as a record of how the pedal came to work the way it does.

1. Motivation

Many effects already react to the dynamics of a guitar or bass. Envelope filters are probably the most obvious example. But the relationship between dynamics and effect parameters is normally fixed inside the effect itself.

Weltgeist approaches the problem differently.

Instead of processing the instrument signal into a particular effect, it extracts information about the player's dynamics and converts that information into an expression-pedal position.

The actual audio signal remains separate. Weltgeist therefore does not need to know whether the connected device is controlling a wah, delay time, modulation depth, pitch, feedback or something entirely different.

The central engineering problem became:

How can the amplitude of an audio signal be transformed into something that another pedal electrically interprets as a conventional expression potentiometer?

2. Inside Weltgeist

Conceptually, Weltgeist can be divided into several stages.

The instrument signal is first observed without making the expression output part of the audio path. Its instantaneous waveform is then transformed into a slowly changing control signal representing the dynamics of the performance.

That control signal is shaped by the front-panel controls before finally driving the circuit that emulates the expression potentiometer.

From waveform to envelope

A guitar signal alternates continuously between positive and negative voltage. That is useful for audio, but not particularly useful when the quantity we are interested in is simply how strong the signal currently is.

Weltgeist therefore transforms the alternating waveform into a unidirectional representation of its magnitude.

One way of visualising this is to imagine the negative half of a sine wave being folded upwards. Instead of positive and negative excursions cancelling each other, both halves now contribute to the detected signal level.

Rise and Fall

The resulting waveform is still far too fast to directly control an expression output. It therefore has to be converted into an envelope.

Rise determines how quickly that envelope follows an increasing signal level. Fall determines how quickly it returns when the signal becomes quieter.

These controls do more than merely change the subjective speed of the response.

If the Rise time is long relative to the duration of a transient, the envelope may never reach the peak that it would reach with a faster setting. Similarly, the Fall setting determines how much of the previous signal level remains when the next note arrives.

As a result, Rise and Fall also influence the effective sensitivity of Weltgeist. Sensitivity, Rise and Fall are therefore related rather than completely independent parameters.

Sensitivity

Different instruments, pickups and playing styles produce very different signal levels.

Sensitivity determines how strongly the detected envelope is translated into movement of the expression output.

A low setting requires a comparatively large change in playing dynamics to move through the available expression range. A high setting causes much smaller changes in the input signal to create much larger movements.

Base

Base determines the resting position from which the dynamic movement begins.

This is useful because many musical applications do not require the full expression range. A player may, for example, want dynamics to move a parameter only through the upper part of its available range.

Together, Base and Sensitivity define the range of expression movement, while Rise and Fall determine how that movement evolves over time.

Emulating an expression potentiometer

Producing a control voltage would have been comparatively easy. But a conventional expression input does not necessarily expect a control voltage.

In many devices, an expression pedal is simply a potentiometer connected through a TRS cable.

Weltgeist therefore has to behave electrically like a variable resistance network rather than merely generating a voltage.

This led to one of the more unusual parts of the design: two optically controlled resistors.

Two NSL-32s and an unexpected result

The resistance of an NSL-32 does not change linearly with its LED drive.

Quite the opposite. The relationship between LED current, illumination and LDR resistance is strongly nonlinear.

Initially, this appeared to be a problem. A strongly nonlinear resistance seemed likely to produce an equally nonlinear expression response.

Weltgeist, however, does not use a single variable resistor. Two NSL-32s form the two sides of the emulated potentiometer and are driven in opposite directions.

As one resistance decreases, the other increases.

The important quantity for the resulting expression position is therefore not either resistance in isolation, but the relationship between them.

In simplified form, the position of such a divider can be represented as:

RA / (RA + RB)

The exact orientation depends on the expression wiring, but the important observation remains the same: the result depends on a ratio.

This produced one of the more surprising measurements during the development of Weltgeist.

Although the resistance curves of the individual optocouplers are highly nonlinear, their opposing behaviour produces a much more linear movement of the resulting potentiometer position.

In other words, the nonlinear components do not become linear. Their nonlinearities happen to interact in a useful way when they are used together.

3. Result and possible improvements

The resulting circuit turns the dynamics of the instrument into a continuously varying resistance network that can be interpreted by compatible devices as an expression pedal.

The behaviour is not mathematically perfect, nor does it need to be. What matters is that the resulting movement is predictable, musically useful and sufficiently close to the behaviour expected from a conventional expression control.

Measurements of the current prototype suggest that the complementary optocoupler arrangement provides a considerably more useful transfer characteristic than the behaviour of either optocoupler would suggest on its own.

Component variation

Optocouplers are analogue components and their characteristics vary from device to device. One area of continued testing is therefore how much the resulting expression curve varies across different NSL-32 pairs.

Calibration

Another question during development has been how much calibration should be performed inside each pedal and how much behaviour can instead be defined by fixed component values.

A design with fewer calibration points is simpler to manufacture and potentially more consistent, but only if component tolerances allow it.

Compatibility

Expression inputs are not completely standardised. Different manufacturers may use different potentiometer values, wiring conventions or internal circuits.

Continued testing therefore focuses not only on the transfer characteristic of Weltgeist itself, but also on how that characteristic behaves when connected to real-world expression inputs.

4. What changed along the way

Weltgeist did not begin with the circuit it has today.

Several parts of the design changed because measurements behaved differently from what I initially expected.

Some stages that appeared nonlinear in isolation became much more predictable when considered as part of the complete system. Other controls turned out to interact more strongly than their labels might initially suggest.

The relationship between Rise, Fall and effective sensitivity is one example. The behaviour of the two optocouplers is another.

This is also why much of the development process has consisted of measuring the actual circuit rather than trying to force it to behave like an idealised model.

Future revisions of this page will document some of those measurements and discarded approaches in more detail.

It is not intended as a complete circuit description, service manual or construction guide.
